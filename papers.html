Digging abroad: Environmental Regulation, Production Relocation and Externalities in the Mining Sector
▸ abstract ▸ figures paper

How do firms operating in multiple countries (MNEs) respond to environmental regulation? I answer this question for the metallic mining industry exploiting a comprehensive proprietary mine dataset and a US regulation as a case to look at the causal effects. I find that more exposed US mines reduced production relative to less exposed ones, while their foreign mines increased production by a sim- ilar magnitude relative to comparable mines owned by firms without US operations. This adjustment happened at the intensive margin and was particularly concentrated in low- income host countries. Restricting attention to firms producing the same mineral inside and outside the US, the within-firm responses is similar in magnitude to the domestic de- cline, which shed lights on how the adjustment took place within firms rather than between them. Next, I look at the environmental consequences of the described firms' resposes. Exploiting data on the production network and the exact location of mining units, I show that local externalities move with production: forest loss and PM2.5 fall around US mines, while abroad PM2.5 increases less than proportionally to production. As mining MNEs are less emission-intensive than domestic ones in low-income countries, the net en- vironmental effect is ambiguous: pollution rises through a scale effect but falls through a composition effect. This result gives a more nuanced picture than the pollution haven hy- pothesis alone.

Land reform, human capital and labor markets in Peru.
(with José Galdo)
▸ abstract ▸ figures paper

This paper studies the medium- and long-run effects of a large-scale land redistribution program on human capital accumulation and local labor market composition in Peru. Using six rounds of population censuses and two identification strategies (differences-in-differences and instrumental variables), we find that districts with higher exposure to the land reform experienced a reduction in illiteracy, an effect that persists for nearly four decades after the reform. This reduction is accompanied by increases in school attendance and virtually no changes on schooling supply (number of primary schools, teachers), suggesting that the effect operates through demand for schooling rather than through the supply of schools. We then examine whether this reduction in illiteracy is associated with sectoral shifts out of agriculture. We find that the share of the population working in agriculture fell in more exposed districts, while the share working in industry rose and the services sector was unaffected. Exploiting heterogeneity in our results, we find suggestive evidence that these effects are concentrated in districts with greater exposure to coercive labor arrangements under the \textit{hacienda} system, rather than in districts where land was more concentrated.

Incentive and Selection effects of doubling mayors' salaries in Peru
(with Victor Gamarra)
▸ project description ▸ figures

Should we pay elected politicians more? Economic incentives affect politicians through two channels: incentives (holding fixed who holds office) and selection (who runs for office). The two operate simultaneously and are difficult to disentangle. We exploit a Peruvian reform that, on average, doubled mayors' salaries one year into their term, with the increase set by population thresholds. The timing of the reform allows us to first look at the isolated incentive effects, then the selection effect from in the following election (re-election is banned), and both combined on the elected candidates. We plan to use the reduced-form RDD estimates to estimate a simple structural model, which we can use to run counterfactuals on how different wage settings affects the candidate pool, and the performance of elected mayors.
